The Human Body

Balancing Realism and Computational Feasibility

For the modeled body to be accurate we can derive our data from modern imaging techniques such as Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI). The IT'IS foundation has such models available for researchers, namely Duke, Billie, Thelonious and Ella. For our Proof of Concept we make use of Duke.

The Duke Model

The Duke model consists of 610 x 310 x 1860 voxels of 1mm, which are labeled for 77 tissue types. In order to preprocess the model, a boundary detection algorithm is applied to detect any missing labels. Upon subjective manual confirmation, the missing voxels are imputed based on the surrounding labels.

Segmentation

Subsequently, the voxels are allocated to various body segments, namely: feet, legs, thighs, pelvis, abdomen, thorax, hands, fore arms, arms, neck, lower head, and upper head. The allocation is performed by a combination of automatic processing (boundary detection) and manual separation.

Surface Extraction

After the segmentation has been performed (Figure 2), the marching cube algorithm is applied to the 3D voxel model. The produced triangular surfaces are allocated to the segment of the nearest voxel.

Analysis of the Retrieved Data

Based on the segmentation and the provided tissue labels, we can visualize the segmental distribution of tissues. The number of tissues per segment ranges from six for the hands to 37 for the lower half of the head. Figure 3 provides an example for one of the more homogenous segments; the feet.
