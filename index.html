Modeling the human body

Human physiology experiments are generally slow, expensive or unsafe. A reliable physiological model makes it possible to explore those questions in-silico first . Thereby, reducing costs and significantly speeding up research and development processes.

Existing solutions

Today's models of human physiology are mostly a collection of statistical models of isolated mechanisms. The models miss variables that matter and only work within the conditions they were developed on. That is hardly surprising: many variables cannot be measured, data from extreme conditions is scarce, and every physiological measurement is inherently noisy. Together, this makes it nearly impossible for a purely statistical approach to predict a system of trillions of interacting cells outside familiar conditions.

Consequences

  • Low generalizability
    Limited data combined with purely statistical models leads to large errors outside the training context. Research shows a 1.7–2.8× increase in error with only a slight shift in exercise intensity (Psikuta et al., 2011).
  • New scenarios cannot be modelled
    Conditions that were never measured cannot be predicted. A model fitted on standard conditions has no way to predict thermophysiological responses during, for example, severe blood loss.
  • Limited understanding
    Statistical models can predict, but they do not explain. It remains unclear why a result occurs or what would change it. Questions like Why did the model predict this? and What should we change to improve the outcome? remain unanswered.

Our approach

Instead of learning patterns from limited data, we build a mechanistic model: a model based on the physical and physiological laws that govern the body, such as pressure laws, the laws of heat transfer, and thermoregulatory control pathways. It combines three ingredients:

Mechanistic models

Physical and physiological laws hold in every condition, so the model keeps working beyond the data it was built on.

Domain knowledge

Decades of physiological research describe how the body's systems interact, so we model them as one whole, not in isolation.

Machine learning

Where mechanisms are still unknown or unmeasurable, data-driven methods fill the gaps within a physically consistent model.
